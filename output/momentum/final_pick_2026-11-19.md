## One-paragraph thesis

CF is the only name in today's field that combines a verified physical shortage with a moat no customer can route around, at a price that still reflects scepticism. Since hostilities began in the Middle East in late February 2026 the world has lost roughly nine million metric tons of urea supply, more than 35% of European ammonia capacity was curtailed in October 2026, and CF itself is running at 97% ammonia utilisation as the clear North American leader with ~40% of capacity — and no new nitrogen plant can be built in under 3–4 years. Farmers cannot in-source nitrogen and there is no substitute at scale, so unlike the AI names in this field the shortage cannot be bypassed by a hyperscaler building its own version. At 10.1x forward earnings with only +9.0% analyst upside — the lowest in the field — the market is pricing a cyclical commodity stock, not the tollbooth on a multi-year supply deficit management guides as tight through 2026 into 2027.

## The shortage + backlog evidence

- **~9M metric tons of urea supply lost** since Middle East hostilities began in late February 2026 (CEO Tony Will, Q3 2026 call, Nov 5, 2026 — verified Phase 3.5). The Middle East normally accounts for 35–40% of globally traded urea and 25–30% of traded ammonia; regional urea exports have been cut roughly in half.
- **>35% of European ammonia capacity curtailed** in October 2026 on high natural gas costs (37% offline or at reduced rates per Q3 release coverage — verified), and Trinidad is permanently shutting down 35% of its ammonia capacity.
- **CF at 97% ammonia utilisation** for the first nine months of 2026 (98% in H1), producing ~7.2M tons of ammonia in that period and guiding ~9.5M tons for FY2026. Its UAN fill program shipped nearly 2M tons in Q3 2026, the largest quarterly UAN volume this decade. CF delayed a maintenance turnaround at Donaldsonville to supply ~100,000 extra tons of granular urea.
- **Demand is firm:** India is expected to import more than 10M metric tons of urea in 2026; nitrogen purchases rebounded in Q3 across the Americas, Europe and India on higher corn and wheat prices.
- **No new supply for years:** new nitrogen plants take 3–4 years to build. CF expects the global nitrogen supply-demand balance to "remain constructive for producers operating in low-cost and low-risk North America in the near- and longer-terms."
- Fertilizer producers report no order backlog; utilisation, forward sales and fill programs are the analogue, and all three point the same way.

## Why it's the category leader

CF holds ~40% of North American nitrogen production capacity (39% of US domestic ammonia capacity per farmdoc daily, May 26, 2026 — verified; the dossier's "~23%" figure for Nutrien was corrected by verification to 16% of US ammonia capacity, which widens CF's lead). Nutrien is next at ~16% of US ammonia capacity, Koch ~10%, Yara ~6%. Farm Action estimates CF captured ~80% of ammonia, 56% of urea and 52% of UAN fertilizer sales to American farmers. The top four firms hold ~70% of US ammonia capacity. CF's Donaldsonville, Louisiana complex is the world's largest ammonia production complex. Q3 2026: net sales $1.86B, +12.1% YoY (beat ~$1.76–1.79B consensus); adjusted EBITDA $833M (vs $667M a year ago); EPS $2.87, +31% YoY.

## Why it's irreplaceable

Nitrogen is produced in multi-billion-dollar Haber-Bosch plants fed by cheap natural gas, with rail/barge logistics and safety certification, and a 3–4 year build time. No farmer, co-op or government can in-source it at scale, and there is no substitute for nitrogen in high-yield corn/wheat farming at scale (biological fixation and precision application reduce use at the margin only). CF's moat is scale + low-cost US natural gas + the largest installed asset base, plus an emerging low-carbon edge (Donaldsonville carbon capture; ~10% of H1 2026 ammonia volumes sold as low-carbon at a premium of over $20/ton; Blue Point One JV with JERA/Mitsui, ~$3.7B total cost, 1.5M tons nitrogen-equivalent capacity, completion expected 2029). The honest caveat: the threat to CF is not disintermediation but **supply restoration** — a ceasefire or restored Middle East/European output closes the ~9M-ton hole and prices fall. That is a different risk from the doctrine's trap, and it is the bear case below, not a veto.
