## One-paragraph thesis

GE Aerospace is the only name in the set that scores 9/10 on both shortage and irreplaceability, and it earns both: through CFM it is sole-source on every 737 MAX and sole-source with the GE9X on the 777X, with an installed base of ~50,000 commercial plus ~30,000 military engines and services at ~70% of revenue — a decades-long, high-margin aftermarket annuity. The shortage is visible in the output data itself: LEAP delivery growth slowed to ~5% in Q3 (535 units) after +26% year-to-date because castings cap output, while Q3 orders jumped 49% to \$19.0bn and remaining performance obligations stand at \$180.4bn. GE is buying the bottleneck itself with the \$11.75bn acquisition of Consolidated Precision Products (close expected H2 2027), so easing the constraint directly converts backlog into revenue — a certified, sole-source engine franchise with a decade-long aftermarket tail, at a price that barely clears the EV guardrail.

## Why it's the category leader

\#1 in commercial jet engines via CFM International (50/50 JV with Safran). LEAP-1B is the **sole engine on every Boeing 737 MAX (100%)**; LEAP holds ~55–76% of the A320neo family vs the grounded P&W GTF; LEAP was 1,802 of 2,386 commercial engines delivered in 2025 (~75%). GE9X is sole-source on the Boeing 777X. Services are ~70% of revenue — the engine sale is the entry ticket to ~25 years of overhaul and spare-parts revenue. Spare-parts revenue is growing 20%+ with delinquencies rising — demand exceeds supply even in the aftermarket.

## Why it's irreplaceable

Airlines and airframers cannot in-source a jet engine: FAA/EASA certification takes a decade, single-crystal turbine-blade casting is mastered by a handful of firms on earth (Precision Castparts Corp. — PCC, Berkshire-owned — plus Howmet control ~80% of investment-casting capacity), and each engine locks in ~25 years of aftermarket revenue. Boeing has flown CFM exclusively on every 737 since the 1980s. The only "substitute" is the rival GTF, which is itself grounded by its own defect. GE is vertically integrating the bottleneck instead of waiting for it: the \$11.75bn acquisition of Consolidated Precision Products (announced Sep 8, 2026; \$7bn cash + debt; close expected H2 2027) buys the \#3 caster, which supplies ~25% of GE's airfoil needs. The honest deduction: the moat is shared with Safran (50/50 CFM) and depends on Boeing/Airbus production rates — GE does not own it outright. **Irreplaceability 9/10, disintermediation risk low.**
