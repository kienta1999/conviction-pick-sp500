## LRCX — Lam Research Corporation (etch & deposition equipment)

- Management raised calendar-2026 WFE three times in 2026: $135B (Jan) → $140B with upside bias (Apr) → **low-$150B range with upside bias** (29 Jul 2026, FQ4 call), saying fab-readiness/cleanroom constraints, not demand, are what hold WFE below the ~$150B+ the chip revenue math would imply — i.e. demand for equipment exceeds the industry's ability to absorb it.
- CFO Doug Bettinger (Citi conference, 9 Sep 2026, via press): 2nm GAA needs more etch/deposition; every 100k wafer starts of that capacity adds ~$1B to Lam's served market; Lam's gross margin at a 20-year high.
- Advanced packaging revenue guided to >70% YoY growth potential (management commentary summarised Aug 2026); NAND systems revenue doubled QoQ in the June quarter.

### 2. Backlog / order book
- **Formal backlog: not found** — Lam does not report an order backlog figure. Proxies from the FQ4 FY26 release (quarter ended 28 Jun 2026): **deferred revenue $2.43B**, up from $2.22B in March, +$213M QoQ, driven by customer downpayments; plus ~**$490.2M** of estimated future revenue in shipments to Japan customers held in inventory at cost pending acceptance (vs $434.3M in March). Combined visible pre-recognition book ≈ $2.9B — small vs a $8.1B single-quarter guide, so visibility rests on the WFE outlook and customer behaviour (management, Jul 2026: customers with multiyear fab plans are "working with us to secure equipment orders" for cleanroom space as it comes online), not on a disclosed backlog.

### 3. Category position — #1 in etch, #2 in WFE overall
- Etch: ~39–45% global share (multiple analyst/press summaries; ~40–50% in key segments), #1, ahead of Tokyo Electron and Applied Materials. Deposition: strong #2-type position (~17% in one summary) behind Applied Materials.

Screen: sub-industry rank 1 in Semiconductor Materials & Equipment, 1.08× the #2 by market cap.

### 4. Irreplaceability / disintermediation — score 8/10
Moat: Fabs cannot in-source etch/deposition tools — no chipmaker builds its own leading-edge etch fleet; the supplier set is a tight oligopoly (Lam / AMAT / Tokyo Electron) protected by process-recipe co-development with customers, atomic-level process IP, installed-base service lock-in (Customer Support was a record >$2.4B in the June quarter), and qualification cycles that make a qualified etch process effectively designed-in for a node's life. Concrete threat is *peer* substitution, not customer in-sourcing: AMAT/TEL can win individual process steps node-by-node, and Lam's share must be re-won at each transition (GAA, backside power, 400+ layer NAND) — it is currently winning (SAM expanding, third straight year of WFE outperformance guided), but the moat is execution-renewed, not permanent.

### 7. Shortage score: 7/10
**Verdict:** No sold-out book of its own, but the cleanest second-order shortage play here — #1 in etch, SAM expanding, WFE raised three times in seven months to low-$150B, and a +20% QoQ guide into the September quarter.

## LLY — Eli Lilly and Company (Pharmaceuticals)

### 4. Irreplaceability / disintermediation — score 9/10

Score 9, not 10, because the moat protects one franchise that still supplies ~96% of revenue (Q2: CF $3.208B of $3.334B total).

## EW — Edwards Lifesciences Corporation (Health Care Equipment / structural heart)

### 1. Shortage thesis — YES, but the shortage is untreated patients and procedure capacity, not valves
Driver: severe aortic stenosis (AS) and mitral/tricuspid disease are massively under-diagnosed and under-referred relative to the therapy Edwards sells, and until Sep 2026 Medicare would not even pay to treat the asymptomatic part of the pool.
- Scale of the untreated pool: AS affects >2.5M people in the US, ~5–10% of older adults; a multicentre AHA Target: AS registry report (published in *Circulation*, covered 28 Jul 2026) found up to ~50% of patients with severe symptomatic AS do not receive aortic valve replacement in a timely manner despite a guideline standard of treatment within 90 days — and no drug alternative exists; the valve must be mechanically replaced.
- The asymptomatic unlock: FDA approved SAPIEN 3 for asymptomatic severe AS on 1 May 2025 on the EARLY TAVR trial (early TAVR beat watchful waiting; composite of death/stroke/unplanned CV hospitalisation 26.8% vs 45.3% at median 3.8 years in the original report). CMS finalised Medicare coverage for asymptomatic TAVR on **11 Sep 2026** (final national coverage determination; asymptomatic patients remain under coverage-with-evidence-development) — sixteen months of approved-but-unpaid demand released (secondary coverage, Sep 2026).
- Procedure, not product, is the bottleneck: treatment requires an echo diagnosis, heart-team referral and cath-lab capacity. Edwards' own Q2 commentary leaned on referral/diagnosis initiatives and guideline updates (ESC/EACTS in Europe) rather than device supply. TMTT is the same shape: mitral/tricuspid disease is common, historically mostly untreated because surgery was too risky, and transcatheter options are only now arriving.
- Demand evidence: Q2 2026 (23 Jul 2026, company release) sales +13.6% reported / +12.5% constant-currency to $1.74B; TAVR +11.3% / +10.5% cc to $1.26B; TMTT $195.9M, +44.8% YoY — growth re-accelerating at the market leader is consistent with a penetration shortage, not share theft alone.

### 2. Backlog / order book
- **Formal backlog: not found** — Edwards does not disclose an order backlog in its Q2 2026 release or guidance, and no primary backlog figure was found for this run. Device sales ship to procedure schedules, weeks not years.
- Proxies (labelled): FY26 guidance raised 23 Jul 2026 — total constant-currency sales growth **10–11%** (from 9–11%), TAVR cc growth **8–9%** (from 7–9%), TMTT sales **$760–780M** (from $740–780M), adjusted EPS reaffirmed $2.95–3.05 (+17% at midpoint); secondary summaries put implied FY26 sales at ~$6.6–6.9B. Management's longer TMTT target is $2B by 2030 (secondary summaries of company commentary). Visibility is therefore guidance-grade (quarters), not backlog-grade (years) — a genuine doctrine deduction.

### 3. Category position — #1 in TAVR, share disputed by definition; #1 pure-play in structural heart
- TAVR: Edwards is the recognised leader with the SAPIEN platform (used in >1M patients worldwide per company statements). Secondary estimates vary widely: ~72% US share cited in one 2025 analysis, ~45% global share in another — treat both as source-dependent; the consistent point is #1, with Medtronic's Evolut as #2, Abbott's Navitor a gaining #3, and Boston Scientific currently without a marketed US TAVR system after discontinuing ACURATE neo2/Prime in May 2025 (it has since taken a stake in MiRus with acquisition rights — a future, not current, rival).
- TMTT: Edwards is among the first movers and the broadest portfolio (PASCAL repair, EVOQUE tricuspid replacement, SAPIEN M3 mitral replacement) vs Abbott's TriClip/MitraClip franchise as the main rival.
- Screen context: Health Care Equipment sub-industry rank 7 by market cap (0.27× the leader) — the GICS bucket is dominated by diversified giants (Abbott, Medtronic, Stryker etc.); within transcatheter valves specifically, Edwards is #1.

### 4. Irreplaceability / disintermediation — score 7/10
Moat: SAPIEN is the most-studied valve platform (20+ years of data), with durability/clinical-evidence depth, heart-team training and referral relationships, and first/only labels that take years to replicate — first TAVR approved for asymptomatic severe AS, first transseptal transcatheter mitral replacement (SAPIEN M3, FDA Dec 2025), first expandable paediatric pulmonary valve (AUTUS, FDA 1 Oct 2026). A hospital cannot in-source a transcatheter valve; the substitute threat is peer devices, and it is live: Medtronic Evolut (strength in small annulus, redo-TAVR approval), Abbott Navitor (expanding indications, CE expansion Aug 2025), and a possible Boston Scientific re-entry via MiRus. Edwards' position also increased in Europe partly due to a competitor exit (management, Q2 call) — a tailwind that has now annualised. Bypass risk is low, share-erosion risk is real and continuous — hence 7, not 9.

### 5. Recent catalysts (~3 months)
- 23 Jul 2026: Q2 beat (sales $1.74B vs ~$1.70B consensus; adjusted EPS $0.78 vs ~$0.74) and raised FY26 growth/TAVR/TMTT guidance (company release); stock rose sharply on the print (secondary coverage).
- Jun–Jul 2026 (New York Valves): new SAPIEN durability data and PASCAL registry data (>4,500 patients); EARLY TAVR longer-term update showing mortality/stroke benefit emerging beyond year 2 (secondary coverage).
- 11 Sep 2026: CMS finalised TAVR coverage for asymptomatic severe AS and removed prior hospital/operator volume requirements — the reimbursement catalyst for the asymptomatic pool, though the stock reaction was muted (<1%, secondary analysis noting it was telegraphed since Dec 2025/Jun 2026 proposal) and CED still gates asymptomatic cases.
- 1 Oct 2026: FDA approval of the AUTUS size-adjustable (expandable) pulmonary valve for children — first FDA-approved valve with polymeric leaflets, first designed to expand as the child grows (FDA/company, via HealthDay coverage).
- Ahead: TCT presentations (PROGRESS, CLASP IITR) flagged by the company for late Oct 2026; Q3 print due 4 Nov 2026 (screen: next earnings 2026-11-04).

### 6. Risks
Valuation leaves no slack: screen trailing P/E ~50.8, and secondary analysis in Sep 2026 put EW at ~36× forward vs ~22× Medtronic / ~33× Abbott — the asymptomatic story must convert to procedures, under CED, faster than sceptics expect. Competition: Medtronic and Abbott also benefit from the same CMS expansion (it is a market, not Edwards-only, ruling); survey data have repeatedly flagged modest US share erosion for Edwards as Navitor/Evolut improve. TAVR concentration: ~72% of Q2 sales ($1.26B of $1.74B) is still TAVR, so any US procedure-volume stall (referral rates, hospital capacity, reimbursement tweaks) hits the whole company; TMTT at $196M/quarter must scale toward the $2B-by-2030 target to diversify. Execution/quality: prior device recalls and FDA scrutiny are a recurring theme in secondary coverage; surgical/structural-heart softness and ex-US pricing pressure are standing drags. Screen earnings growth was −25.4% (GAAP, distorted by charges; adjusted EPS guided +17%) — GAAP optics will stay noisy.

### 7. Shortage score: 7/10
**Verdict:** A real, quantified treatment shortage — >2.5M US AS patients, up to half of severe symptomatic cases treated late, and a Sep 2026 Medicare ruling unlocking the asymptomatic pool for the clear #1 supplier — discounted because the constraint is diagnosis/referral/cath-lab capacity, Edwards discloses no backlog, and rivals share the unlock.

## PH — Parker-Hannifin Corporation (Industrial Machinery / motion & control)

### 1. Shortage thesis — YES, the cleanest backlog-led case in this batch: aerospace
Driver: the global commercial-aircraft queue has outrun the industry's ability to build, which simultaneously (a) pulls Parker's OEM content and (b) forces airlines to keep old aircraft flying, pulling its high-margin aftermarket.
- Industry backlog: Reuters Breakingviews (23 Jul 2026) cited IATA data putting aircraft awaiting delivery at >18,000 — ~60% of the active fleet vs a historical 30–40% — with ~1,500 deliveries expected in 2026 still below 2018 levels and one executive view that normality may not return until the 2030s. A Cassel Salpeter report (covered Aug 2026) put the backlog at >17,000 aircraft (~12 years at current rates), engine overhaul turnaround times up from 60–90 days (2019) to 180–240 days, and >$11B of extra airline supply-chain costs in 2025 — engines/components, Parker's world, are the bottleneck. Honeywell Aerospace cut its 2026 outlook in Aug 2026 explicitly on component supply, not demand (Reuters via secondary coverage) — corroboration that suppliers, not airlines, ration the chain.
- Parker-specific conversion: Aerospace Systems posted its **fourth consecutive full fiscal year of double-digit organic growth**; Q4 FY26 (quarter ended 30 Jun 2026, reported 6 Aug 2026) aerospace sales were a record $1.9B, +13.4% organic, at a 29.8% segment margin, with aerospace orders +18% (double-digit in commercial OEM, aftermarket and defence OEM) (company results/call).
- Industrial side is a broadening recovery, not a shortage: North America organic +~5% and International +6.5% in Q4 (Asia-Pacific +16%), with orders exceeding shipments in both industrial regions (management) — supportive, but the shortage claim rests on aerospace.

### 2. Backlog / order book — disclosed, record, and dated
- **Total backlog $12.8B at 30 Jun 2026, a record, +16% YoY** — **Aerospace Systems $8.5B, +15% YoY (also a record)**, and ~$4.3B Industrial (company Q4 FY26 results, 6 Aug 2026; figures consistent across the release summaries and call transcript).
- Order momentum behind it: Q4 total orders +19% on the prior 3-month comparison basis, +12% on a rolling 12-month basis (from FY27 Parker switches industrial order reporting to the rolling basis — use +12% as the cleaner run-rate); aerospace orders +18%, North America industrial +16% (3-month), International industrial +24% (3-month).
- Duration: the $8.5B aerospace book is ~1.1× a full year of aerospace segment sales run-rate ($1.9B in Q4 alone) and is tied to multi-year aircraft programmes plus life-of-fleet aftermarket; total backlog equals ~60% of FY26 sales ($21.5B). This is the only name in the batch with a hard, company-reported backlog number.

### 3. Category position — #1 in motion & control overall; leader in most niches, not a monopolist in any
- Parker describes itself, and is described in market coverage, as the global #1 in motion and control technologies (~$21B FY26 sales). Its own FY26 Q3 presentation is headlined around a #1 position in the motion & control industry, with ~2/3 of sales from customers buying 4+ of its technologies.

### 4. Irreplaceability / disintermediation — score 7/10
Moat: designed-in content. Once Parker's hydraulics, fluid-conveyance, flight-control or filtration systems are qualified on an aircraft programme, the position typically lasts the programme's life plus decades of regulated aftermarket (parts must meet the original certification), which is why aerospace backlog and aftermarket mix compound. On the industrial side the moat is breadth + distribution + application engineering (the "Win Strategy" operating system has taken adjusted segment margin to a record 27.3% in FY26, 28.0% in Q4) and multi-technology bundling no single rival replicates. A customer cannot in-source certified aerospace motion systems realistically; the threat is programme-by-programme peer substitution (Eaton/Moog/Woodward/Safran win individual platforms) and, in industrial, capable specialists (Bosch Rexroth, Danfoss, SMC) taking share line by line. Electrification is a two-edged shift — Parker is buying into it (Curtis Instruments, closed FY26 for ~$1B) rather than being bypassed by it, so far. Score 7: durable and diversified, but re-won platform by platform, with no single chokepoint technology.
