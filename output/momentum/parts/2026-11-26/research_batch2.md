## COP — ConocoPhillips

**No structural shortage of COP's product.** Oil is a fungible commodity in a market the IEA now expects to move into **surplus in Q4 2026**: global supply rose ~2.3m bbl/d in October to ~105.4m bbl/d (recovering Persian Gulf exports, record US output, restart of Libya's Sharara), while global demand contracted ~2.0–2.7m bbl/d in 2026 vs last year (IEA/EIA, via Commodity Board, 26 Nov 2026). What exists is a **geopolitical disruption premium, not a demand-surge shortage**: Brent $94.90 and WTI $83.68 on 25 Nov 2026 (up 3.7% / 2.0% that day), with a very steep backwardation (Dec 2027 WTI ~$72, Dec 2033 ~$55–56) signalling tight *nearby* supply against ample supply further out. Earlier in 2026 Brent surged past $112 (settled $112.57, WTI $99.64) when Hormuz shipments nearly halted — IEA called it "the most severe supply shock ever recorded", >10m bbl/d missing per UBS — but flows have since partly recovered. COP's own Q3 production **fell** 2% YoY on an adjusted basis (see §2), i.e. it is not capacity-constrained by demand it cannot meet; it is price-constrained by OPEC+/geopolitics. The "shortage" is event-driven and reversible: Reuters (26 Nov 2026) notes BMI expects a US–Iran preliminary deal in Q1 2027 that would "tip the market into surplus and trigger sharp declines in oil and gas prices", and OPEC+ meets 29 Nov 2026 to set 2027 policy with the UAE having exited the group.

## UNP — Union Pacific

### 1. Shortage thesis
**No structural shortage; a volume recovery, not a capacity crunch.** Q3 2026 was a record quarter on **6% revenue-carload growth** (2,293k carloads), not on sold-out capacity: freight car velocity 228 daily miles/car (+1%), terminal dwell 19.9 hours, workforce productivity at a ninth consecutive quarterly record with headcount **down 1% against +6% volume** (Q3 release/call, 22 Oct 2026). The growth was mix-heavy — ~75% of volume growth was intermodal — and revenue growth (+15% to $7.2B) was flattered by **900 bps of fuel surcharge** (fuel $2.56 → $4.21/gal YoY, +140 bps drag on OR). Domestic Intermodal had its **fifth consecutive quarterly record** in volume and revenue (+13% volume), International Intermodal +8%, Premium revenue +21%, Industrial +14% on record carloads, Bulk +10%. That is strong demand for western-US freight, but railroads add capacity by running longer trains and hiring, not by a multi-year equipment queue; there is no quoted lead time, no "sold out", no price-increase evidence beyond "core pricing gains" exceeding inflation. The binding constraint on UNP is the economy's goods demand, which is cyclical.

### 2. Backlog / order book
**Not found / not applicable.** Railroads do not report an order backlog or bookings figure. The closest forward indicators are volume trends and the merger pipeline (see §5): carloads +6% in Q3, accelerating from +4% ex-fuel freight growth in Q2; no contracted backlog $ figure, no book-to-bill, no "booked out until" date. Do not treat carload growth as a backlog.

### 3. Category position
**#1 listed US railroad by revenue, #2 Class I overall behind BNSF — and BNSF would stay #2 even after the merger, per UNP.** 2025 revenue: UNP $24.5B vs BNSF $23.4B, CSX $14.1B, Norfolk Southern $12.2B (Good-Investing, data as of 30 Oct 2026). UNP's own merger fact page states: "BNSF has the largest market share of any Class I railroad today and would still be No. 2 after the merger. Where BNSF and Union Pacific compete head to head, BNSF already has 53% of the business. It holds 60% of the intermodal market." The Progressive Policy Institute protest (18 Nov 2026) puts the combined UP+NS at ~40% of US track and ~45% of US rail freight by revenue. On efficiency UNP is #1: Q3 OR 59.1% reported / 58.8% adjusted vs BNSF 63.9%, CSX 60.9%, NS 67.4% (Berkshire Q3 2026 analysis). Network: ~32,000+ route miles covering the western two-thirds of the US (52,929 km per Good-Investing); combined with NS it would be >50,000 route miles in 43 states.

### 4. Irreplaceability / disintermediation risk — score 7/10
This is the highest moat in the batch, and it is geographic, not technological. For a shipper in the western US, there are **two** Class I railroads (UP and BNSF) and no third; building a parallel transcontinental railroad is economically and legally impossible, and 91% of open locations are accessible to BNSF today (UNP merger page) — i.e. the duopoly is the market. Captive shippers cannot in-source rail: they would need track, locomotives, crews, and STB authority. The bypass threat is **trucking** (intermodal's competitor) and, at the margin, nearshoring/reshoring shifting freight lanes — trucks can and do take share when diesel is cheap and service slips, and ~75% of Q3 volume growth being intermodal shows how contestable that segment is. But for bulk, chemicals, autos, and long-haul intermodal, rail's cost per ton-mile is unmatchable. The merger, if approved, would make the network *more* irreplaceable (single-line coast-to-coast, eliminating interchanges) — which is precisely why BNSF, CSX, and shipper associations are protesting it.
**Irreplaceability: 7/10.**

Next steps: DOJ/DOT preliminary comments due **3 Dec 2026**, responses/rebuttal **16 Feb 2027**, final briefs **28 May 2027**; companies expect completion **H2 2027**. >500 customers (representing >30% of UNP volume) have told the STB they support the combination; claimed benefits: 2.1m truckloads off highways/year, $3.5B/year shipper savings, single-line service on >88,000 county-to-county lanes.

### 6. Risks
- **Merger is the thesis and the risk:** STB has not ruled on the merits; the first application was rejected as incomplete (Jan 2026), and BNSF/CSX/shipper opposition is organised. If blocked or heavily conditioned, the re-rating leg disappears and UNP is left as a solid but slow railroad at fwd PE 20.7 — the highest in this batch.

### 7. Shortage score and verdict
**Shortage score: 4/10. Verdict:** A best-in-class duopoly railroad with a genuine moat and a potentially transformative merger — but the "explosive" leg is regulatory (STB approval in 2027), not a shortage or a backlog; without the merger it is a high-quality 6%-volume compounder at a full multiple.

## EMR — Emerson Electric

### 1. Shortage thesis — triage thesis verified or refuted?
**PARTLY CONFIRMED — the shortage is real, but EMR owns the control layer, not the scarce hardware.** The triage thesis was "grid/datacenter electrical-equipment demand (transformers, switchgear, automation) and order backlog". The equipment shortage is documented and severe: Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 survey — large power transformers **~128 weeks** lead time, generator step-up units **~144 weeks** (over 160 weeks by Q1 2026), custom MV switchgear 70–100+ weeks, mainstream MV ~26–32 weeks vs 12–16 pre-COVID; power-transformer prices **+77% since 2019**, MV switchgear +50% since 2021; estimated 2026 shortage **15% for power transformers, 8% for substations** (Wood Mackenzie, 31 Aug 2026); US data-centre capacity 24 GW → 110 GW projected by 2030, data centres now ~40% of the electrical-equipment market vs 2% in 2020. Eaton's Q1 2026 data-centre orders were +240% YoY with electrical backlog +48%; GE Vernova booked $2.4B of data-centre equipment orders in Q1 2026 alone.
**But EMR does not manufacture large power transformers or MV switchgear.** Its exposure is **automation and control for power generation and process plants**: Ovation (power-plant control), DeltaV (process control), AspenTech software, and Test & Measurement. The power demand shows up in EMR's numbers as *control-system* orders, not transformer sales — Ovation orders **+26%** in Q4, power greenfield control-systems activity **~+45%**, power sales **+24%** (Q4 FY26 call, 4 Nov 2026). A secondary source (Intellectia, citing EMR's AI data-centre work) reports EMR "was chosen to automate on-site power generation for a **1.7-gigawatt AI data center**, with orders for its flagship control platform surging 74%" — treat as unverified secondary reporting, not a company disclosure. **Verdict on triage: directionally confirmed (power/datacentre capex is EMR's strongest vertical), structurally overstated (EMR is a second-order beneficiary of the transformer shortage, not the bottleneck owner; Eaton / GE Vernova / Siemens Energy / Hitachi own the scarce hardware).**

### 4. Irreplaceability / disintermediation risk — score 6/10
High switching costs, low absolute lock-in. A DCS (DeltaV/Ovation) is embedded in a plant's safety systems, operator training, and regulatory validation — ripping it out mid-life is expensive and risky, and EMR's installed base plus AspenTech software (ACV $1.7B, +10.1%) creates recurring revenue. **But** every major customer multi-sources or can re-tender at the next greenfield: Siemens PCS 7/neo, ABB, Honeywell Experion, Yokogawa are all qualified in refining/chemicals/power, and EPC contractors (not end users) often choose the platform. No customer can realistically "build its own DCS" — the moat is certification, safety integrity, and 100+ years of installed base, not IP alone — but a determined customer *can* switch vendors at a new plant. The AI/software layer (AspenTech AVA, Nigel AI, Ovation Virtual Advisor) is EMR's attempt to deepen the moat; adoption is early and unquantified in revenue terms.
**Irreplaceability: 6/10.**

- **Competition in AI software:** Siemens, Schneider, Rockwell, and hyperscaler-native tools are all pitching AI optimisation to the same plants.

### 7. Shortage score and verdict
**Shortage score: 6/10. Verdict:** The only name in this batch with a genuine, growing, quantified backlog tied to a documented multi-year equipment shortage — but EMR sells the controls *around* the scarce transformer, not the transformer itself, so the explosive-return case is second-order and already partly priced at ~20x forward earnings; the strongest momentum name here, not a shortage pure-play.
